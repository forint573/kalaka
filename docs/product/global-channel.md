# Phase 2 — The Global Channel

Giving Kaláka makers an Etsy-like route to worldwide buyers. **Durable goods only.**

Not built in Phase 1 — but four decisions must be made now, because retrofitting them is expensive.

---

## The rule

> **Perishable stays local. Durable can travel.**

Bread, milk, eggs, fresh produce and prepared food never enter the global channel. Wool, embroidery, woodwork, ceramics, leather, painted furniture, knitwear, dried herbs and shelf-stable preserves can — subject to food and alcohol law, which excludes more than people expect (pálinka is almost certainly out; honey and jam are shelf-stable but still regulated food).

## Four things to get right on day one

### 1. Schema
Every listing carries a **shipping class** from the first line of code: `perishable-local-only` · `durable-shippable` · `service-local-only`. Plus the attributes an export listing needs but a village listing never asks for — materials, dimensions, weight, production time, made-to-order lead time.

Cheap now. A migration and a re-photography campaign later.

### 2. Reputation portability
A buyer in Copenhagen cannot read a Szekler trust graph. Local standing must be translated into a globally legible credential:

> **Verified maker · 32 completed local transactions over 14 months · recommended by 4 named neighbors · making since 2019**

The provenance *is* the product on a global marketplace. Etsy's weakness is that it is saturated with resellers pretending to be makers; Kaláka's local layer is **an authenticity proof that Etsy structurally cannot produce.** That is the entire competitive argument for this channel, and it is a strong one.

### 3. Gate it on local standing
Global access is **earned**, not granted at signup. A minimum local track record — completed reservations, active months, no unresolved disputes.

Three things happen at once: quality control on what represents the region abroad; a serious incentive for makers to keep their local activity on-platform (see [app-logic.md §5.3](app-logic.md)); and the local network stops being a cost to the maker and becomes their on-ramp.

### 4. Photography and language
A phone photo of a carved gate on a kitchen table does not sell for €180 in Amsterdam. Export-grade listings need real photography and English copy — neither of which a village maker has. Budget for this as a **service the project provides**, not a requirement it imposes: a travelling photographer, and assistant-drafted English listings reviewed by a human. This is a natural, highly fundable line item.

## The barriers, named honestly

None of these are reasons not to do it. All of them will stop the project cold if discovered late.

- **Tax status.** A person selling internationally at volume cannot stay informal. In Romania that means PFA or similar registration, with real obligations. Some makers will not want this, and that is a legitimate choice — the channel must work for the ones who do without punishing the ones who don't.
- **VAT.** EU cross-border B2C means OSS; non-EU means customs and possibly IOSS. Verify current thresholds and the small-enterprise exemption.
- **Shipping from a village.** Carrier pickup in rural Hargita is not Amsterdam. Verify what Posta Română, Cargus, FAN Courier and Sameday actually collect from, and at what reliability.
- **Payments.** Individuals and micro-entities in Romania receiving international payment — verify what Stripe supports for the relevant entity types before designing anything.
- **Returns and damage.** A one-off carved item damaged in transit is not a stock item that can be replaced. Policy needs to be explicit and fair to the maker.
- **Customs and restricted goods.** Food, alcohol, animal products, and anything containing certain woods or hides.

## Shared dispatch — a kaláka-shaped solution

Individual village makers will not each solve packing, labelling, carrier pickup and customs paperwork. They should not have to.

**One dispatch point per community** — a person, or the local association, who packs and ships for everyone. One carrier relationship, one set of materials, one person who understands the paperwork, and a paid local role that keeps the money in the village.

It is the original tradition applied to logistics: one person does the thing the whole village needs, and the village makes it worth their while.

## What this funds

The clean structural answer to sustainability:

> **The local network is free forever. The global channel is what pays for it.**

A commission on international sales — where real money, real logistics and real value-add exist — funds a local layer that never charges anyone for anything. Sponsors and grants carry Phase 1; the global channel carries Phase 2 onward. Incentives align: the platform earns only when it has genuinely opened a market a maker could not reach alone.
